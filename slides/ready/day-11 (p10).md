**Speaker:** HH Bhakti Dhira Damodara Swami Maharaja

## Slide 2: The Root of the Problem
### **Why Do We Criticize and Fault-Find?**

"The person who focuses so much on the faults of others is really trying to put others down so that they can artificially elevate themselves."

"They are really saying, 'You eat too much, but look at me; I don’t eat nearly as much. You sleep too much, but look at me; I am so austere. You are such a poor book distributor; you went out all day and didn’t distribute one book. But I did twenty books in half the time that you spent on the street. You are not a serious devotee, but I am very serious.'"

"People who fault-find have this mood. In reality, they are just trying to broadcast how wonderful they are by showing someone else’s weaknesses and issues."

---

## Slide 3: The Solution

**Principle 10:**  
"My advice is, do not criticize others. Try to rectify yourself."

"What does this mean? Does it mean that people should do whatever they want without restriction? Does it mean that whatever happens is alright? No."

"Instead of faultfinding, a person should go around love-finding, trying to see how to help make improvements and how to better serve others."

---

## Slide 4: Codifying Behavior as a Cry for Love

"One way to remain loving in an unloving or challenging environment is to always codify the behavior of others as a cry for love."

"If we see a person’s behavior as an attack, to that same degree we feel that we have to fight them back or defend ourselves. Nobody can hurt you unless you let them hurt and distract you."

"However, a deep lover realizes that every person is ultimately looking for love. We can see enviousness as a cry for love. We can see jealousy as a cry for love. We can see anger as a cry for love. We can see fear as a cry for love."

---

## Slide 5: Supplying the Missing Love

"When a person exhibits these emotions, we can think, “This person who seems envious or jealous is actually feeling that the love I am getting is not coming to them. They feel left out. They see me as a thief who has taken what should have belonged to them.”"

"When someone gets angry with us, see it as a cry for love. They feel angry because something has caused them to feel uncared for and unloved."

"Try to determine what aspect of love is missing and what aspect of love can bring healing. What is externally causing the person to manifest anger, greed, lust, and envy? Then you can help the person help him or herself."

---

## Slide 6: Why Both Sides Lose in Conflict

"If someone is angry with you and you are angry with them, everyone loses."

"Consider a fight between two people. The so-called winner may boast that he really beat up the other person, but he himself has a black eye, a swollen jaw, and ripped clothes. Although he may boast that he won, actually they both lost. One may have lost a little more, but they both suffered in the process."

"When we get into the mood of adhering to the low consciousness of other people, then we hurt ourselves because we allow them to distract us from our real purpose. We have used our energy in unhealthy ways when our energy could in fact have been used in positive ways."

## Slide 7: Living Example — The Danger of "I Am Right, They Are Wrong"
### **Prabhupāda Challenges Fault-Finding**

> “'Why they don't associate with the other devotees?'  
> 'I think it is difficult for them because so many devotees are envious that Siddha-svarūpa has so many followers.'  
> **'Who is envious?'** Prabhupāda challenged.”

> “Too often we eagerly correct others and yet remain blind to our own weaknesses. Prabhupāda taught me that **criticizing others, even if the criticisms are justified, does not protect one from one’s own shortcomings.** One is often correct about the faults of others but wrong about his own.  
> 
> Thinking *'I am right (and sincere) and the other person is wrong (and insincere)'* is dangerous. To protect our spiritual lives from offenses to other devotees, we should not be callous to their good intentions, and we should also beware of making criticisms that are neither spoken with affection nor truly meant to help. In addition, Prabhupāda implied in this exchange that a repentant attitude gives one a second chance.”

Source: *My Glorious Master* by Bhūrijana Dāsa

---

## Slide 8: Living Example — "Are You Prepared to Rectify Yourself?"
### **Duty Beyond Weakness**

> “'Swamijī, I was asking point in a different way, that we are so helpless. We are born as human beings with inherent weaknesses and shortcomings, like kāma, krodha, moha, lobha [lust, anger, illusion, greed]...'  
> 
> 'Yes, that I know,' Prabhupāda assured him. **'But you can rectify it provided you like. But if you don’t like to rectify yourself, how it can be done? If you are thinking that you have got so many defects, you rectify your defects.**  
> 
> Just like these European, American boys... They have decided that, *'We shall rectify ourself.'* You rectify yourself and you will be able to do. **Are you prepared to rectify yourself?'**  
> 
> Still unable to grasp that rectification was his own choice, the man answered, 'Why nature has given us...'  
> 
> **'Nature has made you a dog. Why should you remain a dog? You should become a human being. That is your duty.'**”

Source: *Transcendental Diary* by Hari-Śauri Dāsa, Vol. 1

---

## Slide 9: Vaishnava Sangha
### **Heart-to-Heart Reflection**

1. **The Trap of "I Am Right":** Bhūrijana Prabhu notes that even when our criticism of someone is accurate, it never protects us from our own shortcomings. Why is the mindset of *"I am right and sincere, while they are wrong and insincere"* so destructive to our own spiritual health?

2. **The Duty of Self-Rectification:** When faced with our own conditioning (anger, pride, lust), it is tempting to play the victim and say, *"This is just my nature."* How does Prabhupāda's piercing question—*“Are you prepared to rectify yourself? Nature made you a dog, why remain a dog?”*—cut through our excuses and demand personal responsibility?

---

## Slide 10: From Hearing to Living
### **An Immediate Heart Offering**

Don’t just hear it — practice it.

"My advice is, do not criticize others. Try to rectify yourself."

**The 60-Second Self-Rectification Mirror:**  
Bring to mind someone you recently criticized, judged, or felt impatient with (even silently in your mind):
- **Catch the Ego Broadcast:** Ask yourself honestly: *“What flaw in them was I using to artificially elevate myself?”* (e.g., judging their sadhana, service, or behavior just to feel more serious or advanced).
- **Turn the Lens Inward:** Drop their shortcomings entirely. Ask: *“What in my own heart needs urgent rectification right now?”*

**Live Zoom Chat Exercise: The Self-Rectification Audit**  
Drop the pointer directed at others and turn it back on yourself. In the chat, confess the ego trick and claim your own rectification:
- *“Catching my ego: I judged another’s pace of service → My rectification: I need to cleanse my own pride and serve without comparison.”*
- *“Catching my ego: I felt critical of someone's mood → My rectification: I need to work on my own lack of genuine compassion.”*

---

***Kartik Sadhana: The Self-Rectification Rule — For the next 24 hours, whenever your mind starts to notice or critique a flaw in someone else, stop instantly and turn the mirror inward: identify one defect in your own heart to rectify, and offer a silent prayer for that devotee.***
